Pull requests, decoded

A pull request is the last checkpoint between an idea and production. This is how it works, what fires the second you open one, every situation you will run into, and how to automate the tedious parts without automating away your judgment.

What a pull request actually is

A pull request (a merge request on GitLab) is a formal proposal: take the commits on my branch and merge them into yours. Git itself has no such object. The nearest native thing is git request-pull, which prints a summary you would email to a maintainer. Hosting platforms turned that idea into a web object that ties together a comparison (base ← head), a conversation, automated checks, and a set of rules about when the merge button switches on.

Fig. 1 strips it to the bones. The bottom-left dot is the commit main sits on today. The bottom-right dot is your work, made on a separate branch. The top dot is what main becomes if the proposal is accepted. The arrow is the pull request: not code, not a branch, but a request with a paper trail.

Branches exist so that nobody ships half-finished or incompatible work to production by accident. The pull request is the door between a branch and the shared codebase, and the door has a person, and usually some robots, standing in it.

Why PRs exist

A PR does three jobs at once, and teams that only notice one of them end up with a weaker process.

It is a quality gate. Someone other than the author, plus a set of automated checks, looks at the change before it joins everything else. That gap between writing and merging is where bugs, missed edge cases and style drift get caught while they are still cheap.

It is a communication channel. The commit log says what changed. The PR is where you say why, what you tried, what worries you and what you would do with more time. Reviewers can check the branch out and run it, which brings more eyes and more kinds of expertise to the work.

It is evidence. Who proposed the change, who approved it, which checks passed, what was argued about: the PR is the audit trail your change-management policy quietly depends on.

The security researcher's version: a PR is the one place where an attacker's change, your automation and a tired human's attention all meet. Almost every control in this post hangs off that single intersection.

The shape of a PR

Fig. 2 zooms out. Your branch sits on the left, main on the right, and the PR is the bridge between them. Two things orbit it. Discussion is the comments, questions and decisions. Follow-up commits are the fixes those conversations produce. Every push to the head branch updates the PR in place: the diff, the checks and the conversation all re-anchor to the new commit.

Every PR, on every platform, is made of the same handful of parts. Knowing what each one is for tells you where to put controls and where an attacker will look.

A description worth reading

The description is the part authors most often skip and reviewers most often wish they had. A good one answers a few questions before anyone has to ask them: what does this change, what problem or bug does it address, what related issues or PRs does it touch, how did you test it, what was hard, and what would you do next with more time. Learning projects and open-source contributions benefit from two extras: what you learned, and what you are still unsure about. On a routine bug fix, skip the "more time" section; not every question earns a place on every PR.

Add screenshots, a short demo, or input and output snippets whenever the change is visible. And add a security section, because reviewers will not think of it unless you prompt them. Put all of it in a template so the questions are asked every time:

<!-- .github/pull_request_template.md -->
## What does this change?
<!-- One paragraph. What behaviour is different after merge? -->

## Why
Fixes #
<!-- The bug fixed, the feature enabled, or the debt paid down. Link related issues and PRs. -->

## How I tested it
- [ ] Unit or integration tests added or updated
- [ ] Verified manually (steps, screenshots or output below)

## Security impact
- [ ] No new external input, or it is validated at the boundary
- [ ] No new dependencies, or they were reviewed (maintainer, age, install scripts)
- [ ] No secrets, tokens or internal URLs in code, tests or fixtures
- [ ] No change to auth, permissions, CI workflows or CODEOWNERS (or owners are requested)

## Challenges and open questions
<!-- Blockers, trade-offs, anything you want a second opinion on. -->

## Learned, and what next (optional)
<!-- Handy on learning projects. Skip on routine fixes. -->

## Evidence
<!-- Before and after screenshots, a demo, input/output snippets. -->

The moment you open one

Nothing about a PR is magic. It is a chain of events, and once you can see the chain you can put controls on it. Fig. 3 is a level-one data-flow diagram of everything the platform does between you clicking "Create pull request" and the merge button changing state.

Read the diagram left to right, then walk through it step by step. The numbers below match the process numbers in the figure.

  1. Before the PR exists: the push. git push sends your branch to the remote (D1). Push-time controls fire here: secret push protection, rules about who can push where, and any workflows triggered by push. Pushing a branch does not open a PR.
  2. Create the PR. The platform records the base, head, title and body (D2) and computes the diff from the merge base to your head. It also attempts a trial merge and keeps the result under a hidden ref, refs/pull/N/merge. That trial merge, meaning what main would look like with your change, is what most CI actually tests.
  3. Emit events. A pull_request event with the action opened goes out to webhooks (chat bots, integrations, security tools) and to the workflow engine. Later pushes fire synchronize, marking a draft ready fires ready_for_review, and so on. By default, workflows on pull_request run for opened, synchronize and reopened.
  4. Run checks. Workflows whose triggers match are queued onto ephemeral runners. For PRs from forks, the token is read-only and secrets are withheld, and maintainers can require approval before a first-time contributor's workflows run at all. Third-party apps report their own check runs. Every result is attached to the head commit, so a new push means a fresh set of results.
  5. Route reviewers. Paths that match a CODEOWNERS entry trigger review requests. Notifications go out, and reviews start to accumulate as Comment, Approve or Request changes, each recorded against a commit.
  6. Evaluate the merge gate. The platform keeps asking one question: are the required approvals in, are the required checks green, are conversations resolved, is the branch up to date? Its answer, driven by your rules (D4), decides whether the merge button is enabled.
  7. Loop. Push a fix and the process restarts from the events step. Nothing you approved or passed before is guaranteed to still count.

Step 3 is where attacker-controlled input meets privileged machinery. Runners execute code, hold tokens and sometimes hold secrets. That is why a whole section of this post is about the trust boundary around it.

What happens next

After opening, a PR moves through a small set of states. Fig. 4 shows the happy path along the top, and the loops that real life adds underneath it.

State by state

Draft. The author wants eyes on a direction, not a verdict. A draft cannot be merged, and code owners are not pinged until it is marked ready. Checks can still run, which is useful: secrets pushed in a work-in-progress commit are still secrets.

Ready for review, then in review. Reviewers leave one of three verdicts. Comment gives feedback without a decision. Approve says this commit is fine. Request changes blocks the merge until the same reviewer, or someone with authority, clears it. Checks run alongside, and unresolved conversations may block the merge.

Changes requested, then follow-up commits. The author pushes fixes. That is a synchronize event: checks re-run against the new head and, if you configured it, existing approvals are dismissed so reviewers have to look at the new code. This loop is the healthiest part of the process and the one most often short-circuited by "just approve it, I'll fix it later".

Conflicts. Main moved on while you were waiting. The author merges or rebases main into the branch and pushes. Resolutions are new, unreviewed code, so treat them as such (more in the use-case playbook).

Approved, then merge queue or auto-merge. Once required reviews and checks are satisfied, the PR can merge by hand, merge itself automatically as soon as everything goes green, or enter a merge queue. A queue tests your PR against the latest main plus every PR ahead of it, which prevents the classic "green on its own, red together" breakage.

Merged, then deployed. The commit lands, the branch is usually deleted, and release automation takes over. Merging is not the end of your responsibility; it is the start of everyone else's.

Merge strategies

"Merge" is not one operation. The three methods below produce three different histories from the same PR, and the choice shapes what git bisect, git revert and your audit trail can see later.

Merge commit preserves everything and makes reverting a whole PR a single operation, at the cost of a busy graph. Squash gives you one readable commit per PR and a one-step revert, but the individual commits and their context are gone from main, which makes bisecting coarser. Rebase gives a straight, linear history and keeps commits separate, but every commit is recreated with a new hash.

The security angle: rewriting merges create new commits, so original commit signatures do not carry over verbatim. If your policy leans on signed commits, decide what provenance you actually need, and check what your platform's "require signed commits" rule verifies after each method.

Review and feedback

A review is a conversation with a deadline. The best reviews are specific, kind and aimed at the code, not the person. The best responses to reviews are curious and quick.

Read in this order

  1. The description and the linked issue. Does the diff do what the author says, and only that?
  2. The tests. They show what the author thinks the change does. Look for negative cases, not just the happy path.
  3. Configuration, dependencies and workflows. Small diffs here have outsized blast radius.
  4. New inputs and trust boundaries. Where does data enter, and where is it validated?
  5. Authorisation and data exposure. Who can call this, and what could they learn from the response, the logs or the error message?
  6. The rest of the logic. Then readability and consistency with the surrounding code.
  7. Longevity. It works today, but will it break something when the next person touches it?

Reviewers: do

  • Comment on specific lines.
  • Give feedback that is actionable, neutral and short.
  • Ask about the reasoning behind a choice before judging it.
  • Point out readability and style improvements where they matter.
  • Think about how the code ages and whether it can break other code later.
  • Look for consistency with the rest of the codebase.
  • Say "we" and "us"; the code belongs to the team.
  • For security findings, name the exploit path and suggest a fix.

Reviewers: don't

  • Be vague ("this feels off").
  • Request changes without saying why.
  • Soften feedback so much that quality suffers.
  • Guess at the author's intent or knowledge.
  • Approve what you did not read because the author is senior, or the PR is big.

Authors: do

  • Be courteous and appreciative; someone spent time on your work.
  • Ask follow-up questions when feedback is unclear.
  • Answer review questions in neutral language.
  • Act on feedback promptly, and say when you have.
  • Keep PRs small enough to review properly.

Authors: don't

  • Get defensive or deflect blame.
  • Assume you know what the reviewer means; confirm.
  • Read criticism of the code as criticism of you.
  • Push large unrelated changes after approval.

The use-case playbook

Not every PR is a tidy feature. Here is what each common situation looks like, what to watch for, and which piece of automation earns its keep. Skim for the ones you meet often, and bookmark the rest for the day they show up at your door.

Feature PR

Looks like a new capability spanning several modules and commits.

Watch for new inputs and trust boundaries: validation, authorisation, logging. Also scope creep; two features in one PR is two reviews wearing one coat.

Automate size labels, SAST, required CODEOWNERS approval.

Bug-fix PR

Looks like a small diff plus a regression test, with the root cause in the description.

Watch for fixing the symptom only. Ask whether the same bug class exists elsewhere (variant analysis) and whether the test fails without the fix.

Automate Fixes #123 to auto-close the issue; a check that source and test files both changed.

Hotfix or emergency

Looks like a minimal diff branched from the release tag, needed now.

Watch for "break glass" becoming habit. Forward-port the fix to main afterwards, or the bug returns in the next release.

Automate a hotfix/* ruleset that still requires one approval and green checks, plus an auto-opened backport PR.

Draft or work in progress

Looks like an early PR opened for direction, not approval.

Watch for secrets or credentials in early, messy commits that have already been pushed.

Automate run secret scanning on drafts too; keep heavy jobs for the ready_for_review event.

Fork PR from a stranger

Looks like an open-source contribution from someone with no write access.

Watch for edits to workflow files, install scripts and build config. Treat the entire PR, including its title and branch name, as untrusted input.

Automate require approval before workflows run for outside contributors; use metadata-only triage bots (see Fig. 6).

First-time contributor or new maintainer

Looks like a first PR, or a helpful newcomer who keeps taking on more.

Watch for trust built over months and then spent in one commit. The 2024 xz-utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094) was a long game against a burnt-out maintainer, not a clever exploit.

Automate label by author_association; require a second reviewer for sensitive paths regardless of who wrote the code.

Bot PR (Dependabot, Renovate)

Looks like a version bump with a generated changelog and a very confident tone.

Watch for malicious or typosquatted packages, compromised releases and install scripts that run on your build machines.

Automate dependency review, release-age cooldowns, and a narrow auto-merge policy (see Fig. 8).

Docs or typo fix

Looks like a README or comment change. Low risk, not zero risk.

Watch for changed install commands, curl | sh lines, swapped download links and badges that point somewhere new.

Automate a fast lane by path (docs/**), a link checker, and a rule that any changed command in docs gets a human look.

Large refactor

Looks like hundreds of files, mostly mechanical, with a few real changes in the middle.

Watch for reviewer fatigue. Behaviour changes hide in the noise, and "LGTM" arrives suspiciously fast.

Automate a size check with an explicit override; ask for mechanical and behavioural changes in separate PRs or commits; use git diff --color-moved.

Stacked PRs

Looks like PR B based on PR A, so each review is small and ordered.

Watch for approvals given against the wrong base and the retargeting step when A merges. Re-check checks after retargeting.

Automate stacking tools (Graphite, ghstack) or plain git rebase --onto, plus required checks on every base.

Revert PR

Looks like a PR created by the "Revert" button, undoing a merged change.

Watch for pressure to skip review. A rushed revert can break dependents just as a rushed merge did. For merge commits, remember git revert -m 1.

Automate alert-to-revert-PR for failed deploys, with the same required checks.

Release or version-bump PR

Looks like a bot-maintained PR that updates version numbers and changelogs.

Watch for the publish step. Merging this PR often triggers a release with powerful credentials.

Automate two-person approval for release branches, build provenance and signing, and short-lived publish tokens.

Security fix under embargo

Looks like a patch for a vulnerability that is not yet public.

Watch for leaks through a public PR title, branch name or test name. Coordinate disclosure and CVE assignment.

Automate with a repository security advisory and its temporary private fork, so the fix is reviewed and tested privately, then publish the advisory once it ships.

CI, infra or Dockerfile change

Looks like workflow YAML, Terraform, Kubernetes manifests or a base image bump.

Watch for the highest blast radius per line changed: new permissions, new secrets, weaker gates, unpinned actions.

Automate CODEOWNERS for .github/ and infra/; lint with actionlint or zizmor; scan IaC.

Cross-team or monorepo change

Looks like one PR touching code that several teams own.

Watch for "someone else will check that". Each owner reviews their slice; nobody reviews the seams.

Automate CODEOWNERS routing with path filters; one designated integrator to review the interface between slices.

Conflicting or stale PR

Looks like a PR whose base moved on. Merging main in, or rebasing, resolves conflicts.

Watch for evil merges: a conflict resolution can add code that is not in either parent. Review the resolution commit, not just the original diff.

Automate "require branches to be up to date", or a merge queue.

Abandoned or rejected PR

Looks like a PR closed with a comment, or left to rot.

Watch for secrets in a closed PR. Deleting the branch or force-pushing does not make old commits vanish from the PR's refs and views. Rotate the credential; do not rely on cleanup.

Automate stale-bot messages that explain what is needed to reopen.

PRs as an attack surface

Treat a pull request as untrusted input to a privileged system. Three things can be hostile: the code, the metadata (title, branch name, description, commit messages), and the process (urgency, social pressure, reviewer fatigue). The riskiest moment is when a workflow runs.

Fig. 6 shows the trust boundary for PRs from forks, and the two workflow triggers that sit on either side of it.

pull_request runs the workflow as defined in the PR's merge ref. Since an attacker can edit that workflow, GitHub gives fork PRs a read-only token and withholds secrets. The catch: PRs from branches inside your own repo do get normal access, so anyone with write access can effectively run code with whatever secrets that workflow can see. Scope secrets to environments that require approval.

pull_request_target runs the base branch's workflow with a read/write token and secrets, so labelers and commenters can work on fork PRs. It is only safe if it never executes anything from the PR head. The failure mode, popularised as a "pwn request", looks harmless:

# DANGEROUS: privileged trigger + attacker-controlled code
on: pull_request_target
jobs:
  build:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@<full-commit-sha>
        with:
          ref: ${{ github.event.pull_request.head.sha }}   # ← attacker's code
      - run: npm ci && npm test                          # ← runs with secrets in scope

Metadata is just as dangerous. This step lets a PR author run shell commands by putting them in the title:

# DANGEROUS: title is spliced into the script before the shell sees it
- run: echo "Title: ${{ github.event.pull_request.title }}"

# SAFER: pass it as data through an environment variable
- env:
    TITLE: ${{ github.event.pull_request.title }}
  run: echo "Title: $TITLE"

Automation recipes

The goal is defence in depth: several imperfect layers, each assuming the one before it failed. Fig. 7 shows where each control sits between a keystroke and production.

1. Route reviews with CODEOWNERS

The last matching pattern wins, and owners need write access. Keep the security-relevant paths pointed at people who care about them.

# .github/CODEOWNERS
*                        @your-org/maintainers
/.github/                @your-org/security-team
/infra/                  @your-org/platform @your-org/security-team
**/auth/**               @your-org/security-team
package-lock.json        @your-org/security-team

2. Enforce the rules with branch protection

This applies protection to main from the command line, so it can be reviewed and re-applied like any other change. Rulesets are the newer, more flexible route; check current settings names in your platform's docs before relying on this verbatim.

gh api -X PUT repos/OWNER/REPO/branches/main/protection --input - <<'JSON'
{
  "required_status_checks": { "strict": true, "contexts": ["tests", "sast", "dependency-review"] },
  "enforce_admins": true,
  "required_pull_request_reviews": {
    "required_approving_review_count": 1,
    "dismiss_stale_reviews": true,
    "require_code_owner_reviews": true,
    "require_last_push_approval": true
  },
  "restrictions": null,
  "required_linear_history": true,
  "required_conversation_resolution": true,
  "allow_force_pushes": false,
  "allow_deletions": false
}

3. A hardened checks workflow

Start with no permissions and grant per job. Pin every third-party action to a full commit SHA (the placeholders below are yours to fill in) and stop the checkout step from leaving credentials on disk. Dependency review needs the dependency graph enabled, and on private repos, GitHub Advanced Security.

# .github/workflows/pr-checks.yml
name: pr-checks
on:
  pull_request:
    branches: [main]

permissions: {}

concurrency:
  group: pr-${{ github.event.pull_request.number }}
  cancel-in-progress: true

jobs:
  tests:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    permissions:
      contents: read
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@<full-commit-sha>   # v4.x
        with:
          persist-credentials: false
      - run: make test

  dependency-review:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    permissions:
      contents: read
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@<full-commit-sha>
        with:
          persist-credentials: false
      - uses: actions/dependency-review-action@<full-commit-sha>
        with:
          fail-on-severity: high

  sast:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    permissions:
      contents: read
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@<full-commit-sha>
        with:
          persist-credentials: false
      - run: pipx run semgrep scan --config p/security-audit --error

4. A triage bot that is safe to run on fork PRs

This is the one legitimate use of pull_request_target in this post. It never checks anything out. It only reads the list of changed paths and adds a label. Create the label first.

# .github/workflows/pr-triage.yml
name: pr-triage
on:
  pull_request_target:          # privileged: metadata only, never PR code
    types: [opened, synchronize, reopened, ready_for_review]

permissions:
  contents: read
  pull-requests: write

jobs:
  label-sensitive-paths:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - name: Label PRs that touch sensitive paths
        env:
          GH_TOKEN: ${{ github.token }}
          REPO: ${{ github.repository }}
          PR: ${{ github.event.pull_request.number }}
        run: |
          gh api "repos/$REPO/pulls/$PR/files" --paginate --jq '.[].filename' > changed.txt
          if grep -Eq '^(\.github/|infra/)|(auth|crypto)|(package-lock\.json|yarn\.lock|pnpm-lock\.yaml|poetry\.lock|go\.sum)$' changed.txt; then
            gh api -X POST "repos/$REPO/issues/$PR/labels" -f 'labels[]=needs-security-review'
          fi

5. A diff sentry you can run anywhere

A read-only script for research and triage. It walks a PR's changed files and flags sensitive paths, invisible or bidirectional Unicode, network-piped-to-shell, dynamic execution, long encoded blobs and actions that are not pinned to a full SHA. It is a smoke detector, not a verdict: expect false positives and treat silence as "nothing obvious", not "safe".

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""pr_sentry.py - read-only triage of a pull request's diff.
Usage: GH_TOKEN=... python3 pr_sentry.py OWNER/REPO PR_NUMBER
Exits 1 if anything was flagged."""
import os
import re
import sys

import requests

INVISIBLE = re.compile("[\u200b-\u200f\u202a-\u202e\u2066-\u2069\ufeff]")

PATH_RULES = [
    ("CI workflow change", re.compile(r"^\.github/(workflows|actions)/")),
    ("ownership or policy file", re.compile(r"(^|/)(CODEOWNERS|SECURITY\.md)$")),
    ("dependency manifest or lockfile", re.compile(
        r"(package(-lock)?\.json|yarn\.lock|pnpm-lock\.yaml|requirements[^/]*\.txt|"
        r"poetry\.lock|go\.(mod|sum)|Cargo\.lock|Gemfile\.lock)$")),
    ("container or infrastructure", re.compile(r"(Dockerfile|\.tf$|docker-compose[^/]*\.ya?ml$)")),
    ("auth or crypto related path", re.compile(r"(auth|crypto|secret|token|session|permission)", re.I)),
]

LINE_RULES = [
    ("network piped into a shell", re.compile(r"(curl|wget)[^\n|]*\|\s*(sudo\s+)?(ba)?sh")),
    ("dynamic eval or exec", re.compile(r"\b(eval|exec)\s*\(")),
    ("long base64-looking blob", re.compile(r"[A-Za-z0-9+/]{120,}={0,2}")),
    ("action not pinned to a full SHA", re.compile(r"uses:\s*[\w./-]+@(?![0-9a-f]{40}\b)\S+")),
]


def api(path, **params):
    resp = requests.get(
        f"https://api.github.com{path}",
        params=params,
        timeout=30,
        headers={
            "Authorization": f"Bearer {os.environ['GH_TOKEN']}",
            "Accept": "application/vnd.github+json",
        },
    )
    resp.raise_for_status()
    return resp.json()


def changed_files(repo, number):
    page = 1
    while True:
        batch = api(f"/repos/{repo}/pulls/{number}/files", per_page=100, page=page)
        yield from batch
        if len(batch) < 100:
            return
        page += 1


def triage(repo, number):
    findings = []
    for f in changed_files(repo, number):
        name, patch = f["filename"], f.get("patch")
        for label, rx in PATH_RULES:
            if rx.search(name):
                findings.append((label, name, "path"))
        if patch is None:
            findings.append(("no patch returned (binary or too large)", name, "review by hand"))
            continue
        for i, line in enumerate(patch.splitlines(), 1):
            if not line.startswith("+") or line.startswith("+++"):
                continue
            if INVISIBLE.search(line):
                findings.append(("invisible or bidi character", name, f"patch line {i}"))
            for label, rx in LINE_RULES:
                if rx.search(line):
                    findings.append((label, name, f"patch line {i}"))
    return findings


if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) != 3:
        sys.exit(__doc__)
    results = triage(sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2])
    for label, name, where in results:
        print(f"[{label}] {name} ({where})")
    print(f"{len(results)} finding(s)")
    sys.exit(1 if results else 0)

6. Daily-driver commands

The GitHub CLI turns most of the PR lifecycle into a few keystrokes. Review untrusted PRs in a disposable VM or container with no credentials, and never run their install scripts on a machine you care about.

gh pr create --title "fix: validate redirect target" --body-file pr_body.md --draft
gh pr checks --watch            # wait for CI on the current branch
gh pr ready                     # take it out of draft
gh pr diff 123 --patch | less   # read the raw patch
gh pr checkout 123              # only inside a throwaway sandbox
gh pr review 123 --request-changes --body "Line 42 trusts the Host header; see thread."
gh pr merge 123 --auto --squash --delete-branch

7. Let bots open PRs, but fence them in

Dependency updates are the biggest source of PRs nobody wrote. Fig. 8 shows a healthy fully automated path, and the one place a human must remain.

Guardrails that keep this path honest:

  • Auto-merge only patch and minor updates of low-risk dependencies, and only when every required check is green. Never for major bumps, for changes to workflow files, or for packages with install scripts.
  • Add a cooldown so brand-new releases are not adopted the minute they appear. Renovate calls it minimumReleaseAge; Dependabot has a cooldown setting. Most malicious releases are found and pulled within days.
  • Do not give the auto-merge mechanism a token that can bypass branch rules. The gates are the point.
  • Have the bot propose SHA updates for your pinned actions, so pinning does not mean freezing.

What to measure

You cannot improve a review process you cannot see. A few numbers are enough. Time to first review shows whether people are blocked. PR size predicts review quality better than almost anything else. Review rounds show unclear requirements or unclear feedback. Stale approvals dismissed shows the control is doing something. Bypass merges should be rare, and every one should have an explanation. Flaky check rate matters because people learn to ignore a check that cries wolf, and then they ignore the real failures too.

Conclusion

Pull requests bring together communication, review and quality control. They give a structured way to share work and invite feedback, and through review they let a team check readability, optimisation and adherence to standards, which is what keeps a codebase maintainable and stable over time.

They are also the busiest trust boundary in your engineering system. Code from strangers, text written by attackers and automation holding powerful tokens all meet at the same button. Design that meeting on purpose: describe your changes well, put the mechanical checks in machines, put intent and judgment in people, and never let the two blur.

Reflect on what you built, what was hard, and what you learned. Take feedback as a gift. Give it as one. That habit does more for a codebase, and a team, than any tool on the list above.

In one breath: a PR is the bridge between a branch and the main codebase. It carries context to colleagues, protects main with an extra layer of review, and works best when the author explains their thinking and the reviewers stay kind, specific and suspicious in equal measure.
